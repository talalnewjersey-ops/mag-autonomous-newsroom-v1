What Is a Newcomer Bank Account?

A "newcomer account" isn't a distinct product with special legal status — it's a marketing package a bank builds around one of its existing chequing accounts, bundling a temporary fee waiver with newcomer-friendly eligibility (no job, no Canadian credit history, no initial deposit required) and, at some banks, an easier path to a first Canadian credit card.

Separately, every bank operating in Canada has a genuine legal obligation — not a marketing perk — to let you open a basic deposit account if you present acceptable identification, even without a job, money to deposit, or a Canadian credit history. This right comes from the Bank Act itself, and it's not limited to newcomers or to any particular bank's promotional program: FCAC confirms "you can open a bank account even if you don't have a job, don't have money to put in the account right away, or have been bankrupt."

The fee waiver itself is a different thing: it comes from a voluntary Commitment on Low-Cost and No-Cost Accounts that 14 federally regulated banks — including Canada's six largest — have publicly signed onto, and that FCAC monitors for compliance. It is not a law, and it is not universal to "all residents" — it applies to newcomers in their first year plus, at each bank's choice, one other eligible group (such as recipients of certain government benefits). Source: FCAC's Commitment on Low-Cost and No-Cost Accounts.

In short: your right to open an account is law. Whether that account is free for a year (or two) is a voluntary commitment the bank made and FCAC tracks — a meaningful distinction if a bank ever fails to honour it, since your recourse differs.

Eligibility and What You'll Need

Across the six banks compared here, eligibility generally covers permanent residents (typically landed within the last 5 years), temporary foreign workers, and international students — TD's official eligibility page is the most explicit, naming "permanent residents, refugees, and temporary residents (students, workers or temporary resident permit holders)" outright. Scotiabank specifically caps PR eligibility at under 5 years in Canada; CIBC requires a work permit of at least 12 months for workers.

You do not need a Social Insurance Number (SIN) just to open a basic, non-interest-bearing chequing account — under the Income Tax Act, a bank is only required to collect your SIN when an account will earn reportable income (interest, dividends), so it can issue you a tax slip. For a purely transactional account, a bank cannot legally require a SIN to open it, though some banks may still ask for one as internal policy, and CIBC's online pre-arrival application specifically does request a SIN (in-branch applications with immigration documents generally do not).

Typical documents requested across the banks compared:

  • Proof of immigration status: PR card or Confirmation of Permanent Residence, a work or study permit (e.g. IMM 1442), or a Refugee Protection Claimant Document
  • Proof of identity: valid foreign passport, or a Canadian government-issued photo ID
  • For pre-arrival applications (available at CIBC and others): a valid passport plus a Unique Client Identifier (UCI) issued by IRCC

Document acceptance is set by each bank's own policy, built on top of a federal minimum standard set by FINTRAC (Canada's anti-money-laundering regulator) — FINTRAC sets the verification methods banks must use, not which specific document combinations any one bank accepts, so always confirm your exact documents with the bank directly rather than assuming a passport-plus-immigration-document combination will automatically work everywhere.

💡 Newcomer Tip: A Bank Account Doesn't Build Credit History by Itself

Opening any of these newcomer banking packages gives you a place to keep your money and often an easier path to apply for a first Canadian credit card — but the deposit account itself does not create a credit history. Credit history comes from credit products (a credit card, loan, or line of credit) being used and reported to Canada's credit bureaus over time. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I open a Canadian bank account without a job or Canadian credit history?

Yes. Every bank compared in this guide explicitly allows newcomers to open an account without employment, an initial deposit, or a Canadian credit history. Separately, under the Bank Act, banks generally cannot refuse to open a basic deposit account for someone who provides acceptable identification — this is a legal right, distinct from any bank's specific newcomer promotion. See FCAC's guidance on your banking rights for the exceptions where a bank can still decline (for example, suspected fraud or an unresolved debt with that same bank).

Do I need a Social Insurance Number (SIN) to open a bank account?

Not for a basic, non-interest-bearing chequing account — a bank is only legally required to collect your SIN when the account will earn reportable income (interest, dividends), so it can issue the correct tax slip. Some banks may still request a SIN as internal policy even when not legally required (CIBC's online pre-arrival application is one example), so confirm with your specific bank if this matters to you.

Is the newcomer fee waiver a legal requirement, or can a bank withdraw it?

It's a voluntary commitment, not a law. Fourteen federally regulated banks — including all six compared in this guide — have publicly signed onto FCAC's Commitment on Low-Cost and No-Cost Accounts, and FCAC monitors their compliance, but it is not a binding regulation the way your basic right to open an account is. Individual promotional offers on top of that baseline commitment (bonus amounts, specific waiver lengths) can and do change — always confirm the current terms directly with the bank.

Does opening a newcomer bank account build my Canadian credit history?

No, not by itself. A deposit account (chequing or savings) is not reported to Canada's credit bureaus. Credit history comes from credit products — a credit card, loan, or line of credit — being used and reported over time. Several of the banks in this comparison pair their newcomer banking package with an easier-to-qualify-for credit card specifically to help you start that process.

Will I become a Canadian tax resident as soon as I open a bank account?

No — opening a bank account has no effect on your tax residency. Canadian tax residency depends on establishing significant residential ties to Canada (most commonly a home here, or a spouse/common-law partner or dependants living here), as defined by the CRA. For most newcomers this happens on or very close to their arrival date, since that's typically when those ties form — but it is not an automatic rule tied to the calendar date you land, and it depends on your individual circumstances. See the CRA's newcomers page, or file Form NR74 if you're unsure of your exact residency start date.

Is my money safe if the bank fails?

Yes, for deposit products. All six banks compared here are CDIC members, and eligible deposits (chequing, savings, GICs) are automatically insured up to $100,000 per separately-insured category, at no cost to you and with no enrollment needed. This does not cover investment products like mutual funds, stocks, or cryptocurrency.

What documents do I need to open a newcomer account?

Typically one proof of immigration status (PR card, Confirmation of Permanent Residence, work or study permit, or Refugee Protection Claimant Document) plus one proof of identity (valid foreign passport or Canadian government photo ID). Some banks offer pre-arrival applications using a passport plus your IRCC-issued Unique Client Identifier (UCI). Exact accepted documents are set by each bank's own policy — confirm with your chosen bank before your appointment.

Conclusion

Every major Canadian bank wants your business as a newcomer, and the confirmed data above shows real differences worth comparing before you choose: CIBC's 24-month fee waiver is currently the longest confirmed offer, TD pairs a shorter waiver with the broadest stated eligibility and a solid confirmed credit-building option, and National Bank's 3-year taper suits someone who wants a longer, gradual transition rather than a hard cutoff.

Two things matter more than any single promotional number. First, your right to open a basic account at all is Canadian law — not contingent on any bank's marketing offer — so no bank can turn you away for lacking a job, a deposit, or a Canadian credit history. Second, the fee waiver itself is a voluntary commitment the bank made, not a legal guarantee, and specific terms (bonus amounts, exact waiver length) change — confirm the live offer directly with the bank before you apply, since even the figures in this guide carry an August 2026 timestamp and banks revise these programs periodically.

A newcomer bank account is a practical first step, not a credit-building shortcut on its own — pair it deliberately with a credit product if building Canadian credit history is a priority for you.
